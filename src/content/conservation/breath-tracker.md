During a manatee health assessment, the animal is out of the water for a timed exam. Someone on the team has to watch respiration the whole time. If a manatee goes too long without a breath, the crew stimulates it — often by wetting the snout — so the animal takes air again.

Jacob saw that on [Belize 2025](/conservation/belize-2025), including the last water day with **Todd**. When respiration paused for more than about a minute, he helped wet the snout to cue a breath. The one-minute mark is easy to miss when several people are sampling, tagging, and keeping the animal wet at once.

He built a **phone app** so that timer is not only in someone’s head.

Tap when the manatee breathes. The app records the time of each breath and an average breaths-per-minute rate for that exam. If a full **minute** passes without a tap, it **sounds an alert** so the team can cue a breath right away. That is the point of the tool: keep the watch continuous, and make the dangerous pause impossible to ignore.

He field-tested it on May 25, 2026, during [Belize 2026](/conservation/belize-2026) assessments out of Placencia. That morning started with dolphins near the boat. The team then completed exams on two males. Jacob ran the tracker through a live capture, logging timestamps while the rest of the exam went on around him.
